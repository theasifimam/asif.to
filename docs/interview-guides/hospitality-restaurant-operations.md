## Category page intro (`TopicCategory.content`)

Hotels and restaurants in the UAE hire all year, and the interview you get depends on the role. Front-of-house roles (waiter, barista, receptionist) are usually tested on how you talk to guests, often with a short role-play. Kitchen roles often include a practical test or a questions round about hygiene and preparation. Housekeeping interviews focus on routine, attention to detail and honesty.

Most employers want to hear three things: that you stay calm and polite when a guest is unhappy, that you follow food-safety and safety rules even when it is busy, and that you are realistic about the schedule. Split shifts, weekends and holidays are normal in this industry, so be honest about what you can do.

Use this guide in three steps. First read the question and what the interviewer is really checking. Then read the sample answer and rewrite it with your own experience: real places, real numbers, real situations. Finally practise saying it out loud in your own words. An answer you have memorised word for word sounds rehearsed, and interviewers notice.

Roles covered: waiter, commis chef, chef de partie, hotel receptionist, housekeeping, barista, restaurant supervisor.

## 1. Tell me about yourself and why you want to work in hospitality

- slug: `hospitality-tell-me-about-yourself`
- tags: `all-roles`, `introduction`
- type: behavioral · difficulty: easy
- followUps: Why do you want to work with us rather than another hotel? · What do you enjoy most about guest service?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you can speak clearly about yourself in under a minute, and whether your reasons for choosing hospitality are real.

**What a good answer covers**
- Who you are and where you have worked or trained (two or three sentences, not your life story).
- One real moment where you served a guest or customer well.
- Why this type of work and why this employer.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"I'm [name]. For the last [period] I worked as [role] at [place], serving about [number] guests per shift. I enjoy hospitality because I like making people feel welcome. Once, a family arrived late and the kitchen was closing, and I [what you actually did], and they thanked the manager before they left. I'm applying here because [one real reason, such as the brand's standards, the type of guests, or the training you've heard about]."

**Common mistake:** reciting your CV in order, or saying "I need a job". Pick one story and one reason for choosing this employer.

## 2. How do you greet a guest and set the tone in the first 30 seconds?

- slug: `hospitality-greet-a-guest-first-30-seconds`
- tags: `waiter`, `hotel-receptionist`, `barista`
- type: conceptual · difficulty: easy
- followUps: How does your greeting change for a VIP or a family with young children? · What if the guest looks upset when they arrive?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you understand that the first impression is part of the service.

**What a good answer covers**
- Smile, eye contact and a clear welcome as soon as the guest arrives (do not wait until you finish what you are doing).
- Using the guest's name when you know it.
- Offering help straight away (a table, a seat, a menu, the check-in desk).
- Following the employer's own greeting standard if there is one.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"I stop what I'm doing, look at the guest, smile and greet them within a few seconds, like 'Good evening, welcome to [restaurant name]'. If I know their name or they have a booking, I use it. Then I offer help: 'Have you booked a table?' or 'Let me show you to your seat'. If I'm busy with another guest, I still make eye contact and say 'I'll be with you in a moment', so they know I have seen them."

**Common mistake:** a greeting that sounds like a script, with no eye contact. Mention that you adapt tone to the guest, such as being quieter and calmer with someone who looks tired.

## 3. A guest says their food is cold. What do you do?

- slug: `hospitality-guest-says-food-is-cold`
- tags: `waiter`, `restaurant-supervisor`
- type: scenario · difficulty: easy
- followUps: What if the guest is loud and other tables can hear? · What if the guest has already eaten half the dish?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you can solve the problem calmly without arguing or blaming the kitchen.

**What a good answer covers**
- Apologise once, sincerely, without excuses.
- Do not argue about whether the food was cold.
- Take the dish away and offer to replace it, or offer an alternative.
- Tell the kitchen quickly and check the replacement is correct and hot.
- Inform your supervisor, especially if the guest is still unhappy.
- Check back with the guest after the replacement arrives.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"I'd say 'I'm sorry about that, let me fix it right away.' I'd take the plate, ask if they'd like the same dish or something else, and tell the chef it needs to be sent fresh and hot. While they wait I'd offer water or bread if that's allowed. When the new dish arrives I'd check the temperature myself, and come back after a couple of minutes to make sure they're happy. If they were still upset I'd get my supervisor."

**Common mistake:** saying "it must have been warm when it left the kitchen". The guest does not care whose fault it was. Fix it first; talk about cause later with the team.

## 4. Two tables ask for you at the same time. How do you prioritise?

- slug: `hospitality-two-tables-need-you-at-once`
- tags: `waiter`, `barista`, `restaurant-supervisor`
- type: scenario · difficulty: medium
- followUps: What if both tables are VIPs? · What if one of them is a large group and the other is a single guest?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you stay organised under pressure and never ignore a guest.

**What a good answer covers**
- Acknowledge both tables quickly, even if only with eye contact or a few words.
- Handle the faster or more urgent request first (a spill, an allergy, a payment).
- Ask a colleague to cover the second table if you can.
- Tell the second table roughly how long they will wait.
- Do not promise what you cannot deliver.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"I'd look at both tables, nod or say 'I'll be right with you' to the one I can't reach immediately, then deal with whichever request is quicker or more urgent. If a colleague is nearby I'd ask them to help. Guests are usually fine with a short wait if they know I've seen them. If I ended up much longer than expected, I'd apologise when I arrived."

**Common mistake:** saying you would "do both at the same time" or that you would finish the first table completely before acknowledging the second.

## 5. What is cross-contamination and how do you prevent it?

- slug: `hospitality-cross-contamination-prevention`
- tags: `commis-chef`, `chef-de-partie`, `waiter`, `housekeeping`
- type: conceptual · difficulty: medium
- followUps: Where do you store raw chicken in the chiller and why? · What do you do if you think something has been contaminated?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you understand basic food safety, not just the word.

**What a good answer covers**
- Definition: harmful bacteria or allergens move from one food, surface, tool or person to another.
- Raw to ready-to-eat is the main risk (raw meat, poultry, eggs, unwashed produce).
- Prevention: separate chopping boards and knives (colour-coded if your kitchen uses them), wash hands between tasks, clean and sanitise surfaces and equipment, store raw food below cooked or ready-to-eat food, keep food covered and labelled.
- Allergens: the same idea applies to allergens, not just bacteria.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"Cross-contamination is when bacteria or allergens move from one thing to another, for example from raw chicken to a salad. I prevent it by using separate boards and knives for raw meat and ready-to-eat food, washing my hands properly between tasks, cleaning and sanitising my station, and storing raw food on the lowest shelf so nothing can drip onto other food. If I'm not sure something has been contaminated, I don't serve it; I tell my supervisor."

If the candidate holds a food-safety certificate, they can say which one and when they took it. Do not claim one you do not have.

**Common mistake:** describing only handwashing. Mention storage, boards and allergens as well.

## 6. What does HACCP mean for your role?

- slug: `hospitality-haccp-for-your-role`
- tags: `commis-chef`, `chef-de-partie`, `restaurant-supervisor`
- type: conceptual · difficulty: medium
- followUps: Which temperatures would you check during service? · Who keeps the records where you worked?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you understand food-safety systems at your own level, not whether you can recite a textbook.

**What a good answer covers**
- HACCP stands for Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points. It is a system for finding where food could become unsafe and controlling those points.
- Examples at kitchen level: receiving deliveries (check temperature and dates), storage, cooking, cooling, hot and cold holding, reheating.
- Records: temperature logs, cleaning schedules, delivery checks. These are part of the system, not paperwork for its own sake.
- What to do when something goes wrong: stop, tell the supervisor, do not serve.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"HACCP is a system that identifies where food can become unsafe and puts checks at those points. In my role I [what you actually do, such as checking fridge and delivery temperatures, filling in the log, and checking that cooked food reaches the temperature set by the kitchen]. If a reading is out of range, I report it to the chef and we don't serve the product until it's been dealt with."

**VERIFY:** if you add temperature figures, use the figures your employer's food-safety plan or the local authority specifies, and say so. Common textbook figures are 5°C or below for cold storage and 60°C or above for hot holding, but requirements differ and a guide should not present them as universal rules without a source.

**Common mistake:** reciting the seven principles from memory without any example from your own work.

## 7. How do you handle a guest with a food allergy?

- slug: `hospitality-guest-with-a-food-allergy`
- tags: `waiter`, `commis-chef`, `chef-de-partie`, `restaurant-supervisor`
- type: scenario · difficulty: medium
- followUps: What if you are not sure about one ingredient in the dish? · What if the guest says it is only a mild allergy?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you take allergies seriously. A wrong answer here is a safety risk, and employers know it.

**What a good answer covers**
- Ask clearly what the allergy is and how severe it is.
- Never guess. If you are not sure whether a dish is safe, check with the kitchen.
- Tell the kitchen team exactly, so the dish is prepared separately (clean equipment, no cross-contact) and delivered to the right guest.
- Do not treat a "mild" allergy casually.
- Follow the establishment's allergy procedure and record what was said.
- If you cannot guarantee a safe dish, say so honestly and suggest something else.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"I'd ask what the allergy is and how serious it is, then I'd go to the kitchen and check the ingredients with the chef instead of guessing. I'd tell the kitchen so they can prepare it separately, with clean equipment, and I'd make sure the dish is marked or handed to that guest by me or a senior. If I wasn't sure the kitchen could keep it safe, I'd tell the guest honestly and suggest another option."

**Common mistake:** saying "I'd tell them it should be fine". Never reassure without checking.

## 8. Describe a time you dealt with a difficult guest

- slug: `hospitality-difficult-guest-example`
- tags: `all-roles`, `behavioral`
- type: behavioral · difficulty: medium
- followUps: What would you do differently now? · How did you feel in that moment and how did you stay calm?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you can describe a real situation with a clear structure, and stay professional about the guest.

**What a good answer covers (STAR)**
- **Situation:** what was happening, briefly.
- **Task:** what you needed to do.
- **Action:** what you actually did, in order. Calm voice, listening, offering a solution, getting a supervisor if needed.
- **Result:** what happened. It does not always have to end happily; say what you learned.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"One evening at [place], a guest complained loudly that [reason]. I listened without interrupting, said 'I'm sorry, let me sort this out', and [what you did to fix it, such as replacing the dish or finding another table]. I told my supervisor what had happened, and [the guest's reaction]. Afterwards I learned to [lesson, such as telling the kitchen sooner when an order is delayed]."

Use a real story. Interviewers often ask follow-up questions about details, and an invented story falls apart.

**Common mistake:** blaming the guest, or telling a story where you were not involved. Keep the focus on your actions.

## 9. You are short-staffed in the middle of a busy service. What do you do?

- slug: `hospitality-short-staffed-busy-service`
- tags: `waiter`, `housekeeping`, `restaurant-supervisor`
- type: scenario · difficulty: medium
- followUps: Would you stay past the end of your shift? · What if you are asked to do a task outside your normal role?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you stay calm, work as part of a team and tell the supervisor instead of silently struggling.

**What a good answer covers**
- Tell your supervisor immediately that the section is struggling.
- Focus on what affects the guest most: greeting, taking orders, serving food, clearing urgent items.
- Ask colleagues for help and offer help in return.
- Keep communication simple and calm.
- Be honest about what you can do. Overtime must follow your contract and the employer's rules; do not promise unlimited extra hours.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"I'd tell my supervisor straight away so they can move someone or reorganise the section. Meanwhile I'd focus on the jobs guests notice most: greeting, taking orders, and getting food out quickly. I'd ask a colleague to cover small tasks, and I'd help them when I could. If I was asked to stay later, I'd say yes if I was able to, and I'd check how the extra time is recorded."

**Common mistake:** saying you would "just work faster and not complain". Silence leads to mistakes, and good employers want to be told.

## 10. How do you upsell without sounding pushy?

- slug: `hospitality-upsell-without-being-pushy`
- tags: `waiter`, `barista`, `hotel-receptionist`
- type: conceptual · difficulty: medium
- followUps: Give an example for a dessert or a drink. · What do you do when a guest says no?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you can help the business earn more while respecting the guest.

**What a good answer covers**
- Know the menu well, including what pairs well and what is popular.
- Suggest, don't push: offer one or two options, not a long list.
- Link the suggestion to what the guest has already said ("You mentioned you like spicy food").
- Accept a "no" immediately and without commenting.
- Never suggest something that does not suit the guest (allergy, budget, diet).

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"I listen first. If a guest orders a main course, I might say 'Would you like to try our [drink or side that suits it]? It goes well with that dish.' I only suggest one or two things and I'm happy with a 'no thanks'. I get better at it by tasting the menu and knowing what I'm recommending, because a guest can tell if I'm just reading a script."

**Common mistake:** describing upselling as pressure or tricks. Employers want guests to leave happy and come back.

## 11. (Hotel receptionist) A guest's reservation cannot be found. What do you do?

- slug: `hospitality-reservation-not-found-front-desk`
- tags: `hotel-receptionist`
- type: scenario · difficulty: medium
- followUps: What if the hotel is fully booked tonight? · What if the guest booked through a third-party website?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you stay polite and systematic when the guest is tired and the system does not match their story.

**What a good answer covers**
- Apologise for the inconvenience and stay calm.
- Search in more than one way: surname, first name, spelling variations, email address, phone number, booking reference, check-in date, and the booking channel.
- Ask the guest to show the confirmation email or booking reference.
- If it is a third-party booking, check how the hotel receives those bookings.
- If you cannot find it, tell your supervisor or duty manager before you promise anything.
- Offer options if the hotel has availability and the manager approves. Never promise a room or a rate you cannot confirm.
- Document what happened.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"I'd say 'I'm sorry for the trouble, let me check it for you.' I'd search by surname, email and booking reference, and check the date and spelling variations. I'd ask for their confirmation email so I can see the booking source. If I still couldn't find it, I'd explain what I was doing and speak to the duty manager, because they can approve an alternative. I wouldn't promise a room until I knew what we had. Afterwards I'd note what happened so the team can follow up."

**Common mistake:** saying "there's nothing I can do" or promising a room to calm the guest down.

## 12. (Housekeeping) How do you prioritise rooms on a busy day?

- slug: `hospitality-housekeeping-prioritise-rooms`
- tags: `housekeeping`
- type: scenario · difficulty: easy
- followUps: What do you do if you find a lost item? · What do you do if a guest is still in the room when you arrive?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you understand how housekeeping supports the front desk and the guest, and whether you follow a plan.

**What a good answer covers**
- Start from the supervisor's allocation sheet or the list from the front desk.
- Normally, rooms needed soon (early check-ins, VIPs, urgent requests) and departures come before stayovers. A guest's "do not disturb" sign is respected.
- Keep the supervisor informed when something blocks a room (maintenance issue, extra cleaning).
- Report lost property and any damage or safety problem in line with hotel procedure.
- Never enter a room without following the knocking and entry procedure.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"I'd start with the list from my supervisor and clean the rooms the front desk needs first, such as early check-ins and VIP arrivals. Then departures, then stayovers. If a guest has a 'Do not disturb' sign, I leave that room and tell my supervisor. If I find something wrong, like a broken item or a maintenance issue, I report it so the room isn't released by mistake. If I find a guest's item, I don't open or keep it; I give it to my supervisor and it's logged."

**Common mistake:** describing how you clean a room rather than how you prioritise. The question is about order and communication.

## 13. (Chef) Walk me through how you would prepare mise en place for a service

- slug: `hospitality-mise-en-place-service-prep`
- tags: `commis-chef`, `chef-de-partie`
- type: conceptual · difficulty: medium
- followUps: What if a key ingredient has not arrived? · How do you decide quantities for the day?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you work in an organised, repeatable way, and whether your hygiene habits are part of the routine.

**What a good answer covers**
- Read the prep list or menu and the expected covers/bookings.
- Check the station: clean and sanitised surfaces, correct equipment, knives and boards.
- Check stock and dates; use older stock first.
- Prepare, portion, label and date items; store them correctly.
- Taste and check seasoning where it is part of your role.
- Clean as you go; leave the station ready for service.
- Tell the chef about shortages early.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"I start by reading the prep list and checking how many covers we expect. I clean my station, set up my boards and knives, and check what I have in the fridge, using older stock first. Then I prepare and portion what the list needs, label and date it, and store it properly. I taste where it's part of my job, keep the station clean as I work, and tell the chef early if I'm short of anything so they can adjust the menu or order."

**Common mistake:** listing only chopping tasks. Include checking stock, labelling, cleaning and communication.

## 14. (Chef) What is FIFO and why does it matter?

- slug: `hospitality-fifo-stock-rotation`
- tags: `commis-chef`, `chef-de-partie`, `restaurant-supervisor`
- type: conceptual · difficulty: easy
- followUps: How do you label a prepared item? · What do you do with food that has passed its date?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you handle stock safely and avoid waste.

**What a good answer covers**
- FIFO means "first in, first out": older stock is used before newer stock.
- It reduces waste and the chance of serving food past its use-by date.
- Practical steps: put new deliveries behind older items, label and date everything, check dates daily.
- Expired items are removed and recorded according to the kitchen's procedure; they are not served.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"FIFO means first in, first out. When a delivery arrives I put the new items behind the older ones and check the dates, and every prepared item gets a label with the date. Each day I check what's near its date and tell the chef so we can use it first. Anything past its date is removed and recorded, not served."

**Common mistake:** knowing the term but not being able to describe what you actually do in the walk-in fridge.

## 15. How do you work under pressure during peak hours?

- slug: `hospitality-working-under-pressure-peak-hours`
- tags: `all-roles`
- type: behavioral · difficulty: easy
- followUps: How do you avoid mistakes when you are rushing? · What do you do when you feel overwhelmed?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you can give a concrete example, not just say "I'm a hard worker".

**What a good answer covers**
- A real number to show scale (covers per shift, tables in your section, rooms to clean).
- Specific habits: preparing before the rush, working in a set order, communicating with colleagues, asking for help.
- Keeping quality and safety while moving quickly.
- What you do if you make a mistake (tell someone, fix it).

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"On a typical weekend at [place] I looked after [number] tables. Before the rush I stocked my station, checked my section and made sure I knew the specials. During service I worked in a set order: greet, take orders, check that drinks and food go out together, and clear tables as I passed. I kept talking to the kitchen and my colleagues so nothing was forgotten. If I made a mistake, I told my supervisor immediately and fixed it."

**Common mistake:** saying "I love pressure" with nothing to back it up. Show your system.

## 16. How do you work with colleagues from different cultures and languages?

- slug: `hospitality-working-in-a-multicultural-team`
- tags: `all-roles`
- type: behavioral · difficulty: easy
- followUps: Which languages do you speak? · What do you do if you do not understand an instruction?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you can work in the diverse teams that are typical in UAE hospitality.

**What a good answer covers**
- Respect and patience.
- Clear, simple language: short sentences, no slang, checking understanding ("Just to confirm, you need table six cleared?").
- Asking when you do not understand, instead of guessing.
- Helping new colleagues.
- Only claim languages you can really use at work.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"At [place] our team came from [number or examples of countries, if true], and the shared language was English. I speak slowly and clearly, use simple words, and check that we understand each other, especially for orders and allergy information. If I don't understand something I ask again instead of guessing. I try to learn a few words in my colleagues' languages, and I help new people with the routine."

**Common mistake:** claiming languages you cannot use in a conversation, or saying "I have no problem with anyone", which sounds empty.

## 17. Are you comfortable with split shifts, weekends and holidays?

- slug: `hospitality-split-shifts-weekends-holidays`
- tags: `all-roles`
- type: conceptual · difficulty: easy
- followUps: How do you handle tiredness after a double shift? · What do you need to know about the roster before accepting the job?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you understand the nature of the industry and will stay in the job. A dishonest "yes to everything" often leads to early resignations.

**What a good answer covers**
- A truthful answer based on your own situation.
- Showing you understand the pattern: nights, weekends and holidays are when guests are busiest.
- Asking sensible questions about the roster, rest days and how overtime or split shifts are scheduled.
- Describing how you manage energy and routines (sleep, meals, transport).

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"Yes. I've worked [pattern at your previous job] and I understand that weekends and holidays are the busiest times. I'd like to know how the roster is set up and how much notice is given. I manage split shifts by [real habits, such as resting between shifts and keeping meals regular]. If there is anything I can't do, I'd say so now instead of promising and failing later."

**Common mistake:** saying yes to everything and then raising problems after you start.

## 18. Describe your grooming and uniform standards

- slug: `hospitality-grooming-and-uniform-standards`
- tags: `all-roles`
- type: conceptual · difficulty: easy
- followUps: Why do these standards matter in a guest-facing role? · Would you follow a stricter standard if the hotel asked?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you understand that your appearance is part of the brand and of hygiene.

**What a good answer covers**
- Clean, pressed uniform; name badge worn properly; polished, suitable shoes.
- Neat hair and nails; any jewellery or fragrance according to the employer's policy.
- Personal hygiene before every shift.
- In kitchens: hair covering, clean whites, no jewellery near food (follow the kitchen's rule).
- Willingness to follow the employer's standard, which can be stricter than your own.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"I arrive with a clean, pressed uniform, my name badge, and shoes that are clean and comfortable. My hair and nails are neat and I keep jewellery to what the hotel allows. In a kitchen I cover my hair and wash my hands before I start. It matters because guests judge the whole place by how we look, and because hygiene is part of food safety. If the hotel has stricter rules than my last job, I'll follow them."

**Common mistake:** treating it as a trivial question. Employers use it to check attitude toward standards.

## 19. What would you do if you saw a colleague breaking a hygiene rule?

- slug: `hospitality-colleague-breaking-hygiene-rule`
- tags: `all-roles`, `commis-chef`, `waiter`
- type: scenario · difficulty: medium
- followUps: What if it were your supervisor? · What if the colleague is your friend?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you put guest safety first and can handle a delicate conversation professionally.

**What a good answer covers**
- Safety before friendship: you cannot ignore something that could make a guest ill.
- If it is minor and safe to do so, speak to the colleague privately and politely first (for example, a quick reminder to wash hands).
- If it is serious (for example, handling food while ill, or a repeated breach), or if they do not stop, tell the supervisor.
- If the person is your supervisor, follow the company's reporting route (their manager, HR or the hygiene or complaints process).
- Do not gossip or embarrass the colleague in front of others.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"If it's something small, I'd speak to them quietly and politely, for example 'Could you wash your hands before you handle that?'. If it was serious or they didn't stop, I'd tell the supervisor, because guests' safety comes first. If the person was my supervisor, I'd report it to the next manager or through the hotel's reporting process. I'd stay respectful and stick to the facts."

**Common mistake:** saying you would ignore it to avoid trouble, or that you would shout at the colleague in front of others.

## 20. Where do you see yourself in two years?

- slug: `hospitality-where-do-you-see-yourself-in-two-years`
- tags: `all-roles`
- type: behavioral · difficulty: easy
- followUps: What training would you like to do? · What would help you reach that role?

**Answer**

**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you want to grow in this industry and whether your goals fit what the employer can offer.

**What a good answer covers**
- A realistic next step in the same field (for example commis chef to chef de partie, waiter to senior waiter or supervisor, receptionist to guest relations or front office supervisor).
- What you plan to learn: skills, certificates, systems, languages.
- A link to what this employer offers.
- No promise to stay forever and no sign that you are using the job only as a stepping stone to something unrelated.

**Sample answer (adapt this to your own experience)**
"In two years I'd like to be [next realistic role] here, having [what you plan to learn, such as the property's systems, a food-safety course, or training in wine service]. I'd also like to take on more responsibility, like training new staff. I know that comes from doing this role well first, so right now I want to learn the standards here and be reliable."

**Common mistake:** "I want your job" or "I don't know". Show ambition and humility together.
